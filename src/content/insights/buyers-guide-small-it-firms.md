Finding the right small IT firm for a government engagement is harder than it looks. The challenge is not a shortage of vendors — SAM.gov lists tens of thousands of IT companies. The challenge is separating firms with genuine delivery capability from those that exist primarily on paper.

This guide provides a structured approach for contracting officers, contracting specialists, program managers, and acquisition professionals who need to find small IT firms that can actually perform.

## Where to Search

The federal procurement ecosystem offers several databases and platforms for identifying small businesses. Each serves a different purpose, and experienced buyers typically cross-reference multiple sources.

| Source | Best For | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **SAM.gov** | Verifying active registration, NAICS codes, socioeconomic certifications, and entity details | No performance data; self-reported capabilities |
| **SBA Dynamic Small Business Search (DSBS)** | Filtering by certification (WOSB, SDVOSB, 8(a), HUBZone), geography, NAICS, and keywords | Data can lag behind SAM.gov; limited detail |
| **FPDS.gov** | Verifying actual contract awards — agencies, dollar values, performance periods | Steep learning curve; no qualitative data |
| **USAspending.gov** | Seeing where contract dollars have gone by agency, NAICS, and vendor | Aggregate data; less useful for individual firm evaluation |
| **GovWin (Deltek)** | Tracking pipeline opportunities and identifying vendors already pursuing relevant work | Paid subscription required |
| **LinkedIn** | Finding individuals behind the company — their backgrounds, clearances, and professional networks | Self-reported; no verification |
| **Agency OSDBU offices** | Getting referrals from the Office of Small and Disadvantaged Business Utilization at each agency | Varies by agency; relationship-dependent |

The most effective approach combines FPDS contract history (what have they actually won?) with SAM.gov registration data (are they current?) and a direct review of the firm's capability statement.

## What to Look For in a Capability Statement

A capability statement is the standard document small businesses use to introduce themselves to government buyers. A well-constructed capability statement reveals a lot about how a firm operates.

### Green Flags

- **Named past performance** — Specific agency names, contract numbers or vehicle references, and measurable outcomes. A firm that names "U.S. Navy SIGINT modernization" is more credible than one that says "supported DoD clients."
- **Relevant NAICS codes** — The firm's registered NAICS codes match the work you need. Look for primary codes in the 541xxx range (Professional, Scientific, and Technical Services) for IT work.
- **Current certifications** — WOSB, SDVOSB, 8(a), or HUBZone certifications that are active and verifiable through SBA.
- **Specific platform experience** — Named platforms (Palantir, ServiceNow, AWS, Azure) rather than generic "cloud" or "IT modernization" language.
- **Personnel credentials** — PMP, CISSP, SAFe, AWS certifications tied to actual staff, not aspirational hiring plans.
- **Security clearance posture** — Indication of facility clearance level (FCL) or individual clearance readiness, if applicable.
- **Multi-domain delivery** — Firms that deliver across cloud, cybersecurity, analytics, and program support under one team reduce vendor management complexity and integration risk compared to engaging separate single-capability vendors.
- **Leadership with delivery experience** — Principals who have personally executed on contracts similar to the ones the firm bids on — not just managed business development — tend to produce more credible capability statements and more realistic proposals.
- **CAGE code and UEI** — Active identifiers that show the firm is SAM-registered and ready to contract.

### Red Flags

- **No named clients** — Vague references to "federal agencies" or "Fortune 500 companies" without specifics.
- **Capability list longer than performance history** — A firm claiming 15 capability areas with 2 years of history likely cannot deliver all of them at scale.
- **No past performance outside subcontracting** — While subcontracting is valid experience, a firm with no prime contract delivery may struggle with the administrative and reporting requirements of a prime role.
- **Outdated registration** — Expired SAM registration, lapsed certifications, or CAGE codes that don't resolve in SAM.gov.
- **Generic copy** — Language that reads like a template and could apply to any IT company. The best firms sound specific because they are.

## An Evaluation Framework

When comparing multiple small IT firms for a potential engagement, a structured evaluation helps maintain consistency and defensibility. The following framework covers the factors that most strongly predict delivery success.

| Evaluation Factor | Weight | What to Assess |
|---|---|---|
| **Relevant past performance** | High | Named contracts in the same domain, agency, or technology stack. Verify through FPDS or direct references. |
| **Technical capability** | High | Platform certifications, demonstrated architecture experience, named technical personnel. |
| **Socioeconomic certification** | Medium | Active WOSB, SDVOSB, 8(a), or HUBZone status — verified through SBA, not self-reported. |
| **Security posture** | Medium–High | Facility clearance, personnel clearances, compliance awareness (NIST 800-171, CMMC, FedRAMP). |
| **Staffing model** | Medium | Does the firm employ its key personnel directly, or rely heavily on subcontractors? Direct employees indicate stability. |
| **Financial health** | Medium | Adequate revenue history relative to contract size. A firm doing $1M/year may struggle to ramp for a $5M contract. |
| **Geographic presence** | Low–Medium | Relevant if on-site work is required. Less important for remote-capable engagements. |
| **Dual certification** | Medium | Firms holding both WOSB and SDVOSB (or other dual certifications) increase acquisition flexibility — a single award can count toward multiple socioeconomic goals. |
| **Teaming relationships** | Low–Medium | Established relationships with complementary firms or primes that can provide surge capacity. |

## How to Structure an RFI for Small Business Outreach

A well-written Request for Information (RFI) is one of the most effective tools for identifying qualified small businesses before issuing a solicitation. RFIs are low-commitment for both the government and the vendor, and they generate useful market intelligence.

### RFI Best Practices

- **Be specific about the requirement** — Describe the technical environment, the agencies or end users involved, the approximate scope, and any mandatory qualifications (clearances, certifications, platform experience).
- **Ask for capability statements** — Request that respondents include their standard capability statement plus a brief narrative addressing the specific requirement.
- **Ask about past performance directly** — Include a question like: "Describe up to three contracts of similar scope, complexity, and technology that your firm has performed within the last five years. Include agency name, contract value, and a brief description of work performed."
- **Request key personnel resumes** — Ask for resumes of the individuals who would lead the engagement, not the company's general bench.
- **Ask about teaming** — If the requirement may exceed a single small firm's capacity, ask whether the respondent plans to team and with whom.
- **Set a page limit** — Keep responses manageable. A 10-page limit forces firms to prioritize relevant experience over generic marketing.
- **Post it on SAM.gov** — All RFIs should be posted publicly on SAM.gov to maximize visibility. Consider also posting on agency-specific platforms and emailing to your agency's OSDBU distribution list.

## Tips by Role

### For Contracting Officers (COs)
- Use FPDS to verify that a firm's claimed past performance corresponds to actual awards. Contract numbers and agency names are verifiable.
- Consider sole-source authority for WOSB and SDVOSB firms on requirements under $4.5M. This can significantly accelerate acquisition timelines.
- Coordinate early with your Small Business Specialist to ensure set-aside decisions are defensible.

### For Contracting Specialists (CSs)
- Build and maintain a vendor reference file organized by NAICS code, certification type, and capability area. This makes future source selection faster.
- When reviewing capability statements, focus on the past performance section first. A strong past performance section usually correlates with a strong technical capability.

### For Program Managers (PMs)
- Engage with potential vendors before the acquisition process formally begins. Industry days, capability briefings, and one-on-one meetings are standard practice and encouraged under FAR 15.201.
- Define your requirements clearly before engaging with vendors. Ambiguous requirements attract generic responses.
- Think about contract structure (T&M, FFP, cost-plus) before evaluating vendors. Some small firms excel at fixed-price delivery but lack the overhead infrastructure for cost-plus reporting.

## Common Mistakes Buyers Make

- **Evaluating on size, not substance** — The number of employees is a poor predictor of delivery quality. A 20-person firm with deep domain expertise will often outperform a 200-person firm with a generalist bench.
- **Ignoring the subcontracting plan** — On large contracts, the small business subcontracting plan is not just a compliance checkbox. It is a binding plan that affects how work is distributed and who benefits.
- **Relying solely on SAM.gov profiles** — SAM.gov registration is necessary but not sufficient. Registration only confirms that a company exists and is eligible to bid — it says nothing about capability.
- **Skipping reference checks** — Past performance questionnaires exist for a reason. Call the references. Ask about quality, timeliness, cost control, and whether they would work with the firm again.
- **Moving too fast on sole-source** — Sole-source authority for WOSB/SDVOSB firms is a powerful tool, but it requires a documented determination that the firm can perform the work at a fair and reasonable price.
- **Searching only one certification category** — A firm holding both WOSB and SDVOSB certifications lets a single award count toward both the 5% WOSB goal and the 3% SDVOSB goal. Buyers who filter by only one category at a time may miss dual-certified firms that offer compounding procurement value.

## Key Takeaways

- Cross-reference SAM.gov, FPDS, and SBA databases to build a complete picture of any small IT firm
- Prioritize named past performance and verifiable credentials over marketing language
- Use structured RFIs to surface qualified firms before issuing solicitations
- Engage early and engage specifically — generic outreach attracts generic vendors
- Dual-certified firms (e.g., both WOSB and SDVOSB) let a single award count toward multiple scorecard goals — search across certification categories, not within them
- Small firms that name their clients, their platforms, and their people — and whose leadership has personally delivered on similar contracts — are the ones most likely to deliver
