Choosing the wrong IT partner on a federal contract is expensive — not just in dollars, but in schedule delays, recompetition costs, and program credibility. For small business set-asides and teaming arrangements, the evaluation challenge is sharper: many small IT firms look similar on paper, but their actual delivery capacity varies enormously.

The firms that consistently deliver are the ones where certifications, technical depth, and delivery experience reinforce each other — where leadership has actually executed the kind of work the firm bids on, not just sold it. This guide provides a structured approach for identifying those firms, grounded in the Federal Acquisition Regulation and the practical realities of federal IT delivery.

## The FAR Foundation: Responsibility Standards

Before any contract award, the contracting officer must make an affirmative determination of responsibility under **FAR 9.104-1**. This is not optional — it is a binary, pass/fail gate that applies to every prospective contractor. The seven general standards require that a firm:

1. Has **adequate financial resources** to perform the contract, or the ability to obtain them
2. Is able to comply with the proposed **delivery or performance schedule**
3. Has a **satisfactory performance record**
4. Has a satisfactory record of **integrity and business ethics**
5. Has the necessary **organization, experience, accounting, and operational controls** — or the ability to obtain them
6. Has the necessary **production, technical equipment, and facilities** — or the ability to obtain them
7. Is **otherwise qualified and eligible** to receive an award under applicable laws and regulations

The "ability to obtain" language is critical for small businesses. A firm does not need to possess every resource at proposal time — it must demonstrate the ability to acquire them before performance begins. This accommodates the reality that small IT firms often scale up after award.

If a contracting officer finds a small business non-responsible, the matter must be referred to the **SBA for a Certificate of Competency (COC)** determination under FAR 19.602. The SBA conducts its own investigation and can override the CO's finding, compelling the agency to make the award.

## Core Evaluation Criteria

Beyond the responsibility determination, source selection evaluations under **FAR 15.3** assess offerors against criteria disclosed in the solicitation. For IT services, the following factors consistently predict delivery success.

### Past Performance

Past performance is treated as an indicator of future performance quality. Under **FAR 15.305(a)(2)**, evaluators assess the relevance and quality of prior work in areas such as:

- Contract scope and complexity relative to the current requirement
- Quality of deliverables
- Schedule adherence
- Cost control
- Customer satisfaction

For small firms with limited history, **FAR 15.305(a)(2)(iv)** provides a critical protection: a lack of relevant past performance shall not be evaluated favorably or unfavorably — it is rated as neutral. This prevents newer firms from being penalized for their size, but it also means evaluators must look harder at other factors.

Both prime and subcontract past performance should be examined. Many capable small IT firms build their federal track record as subcontractors before competing as primes.

### Technical Capability

| Indicator | What to Look For |
|---|---|
| **Platform certifications** | AWS, Azure, ServiceNow, Palantir, ArcGIS partnerships — verified, not claimed |
| **Methodology maturity** | Agile, DevSecOps, ITIL, SAFe — demonstrated through past delivery, not just training certificates |
| **Architecture experience** | Zero trust implementations, cloud migrations, API-first design, geospatial analytics — with named project references |
| **OEM relationships** | Authorized reseller, managed service provider, or consulting partner status with relevant vendors |
| **Technical staff credentials** | CISSP, PMP, SAFe Agilist, AWS Solutions Architect — held by named personnel, not "TBD" hires |
| **Multi-domain delivery** | Firms spanning cloud, cybersecurity, analytics, and program support under one team reduce integration risk compared to managing multiple single-capability vendors |

### Security Posture

Security is increasingly a threshold requirement — not a scored factor, but a pass/fail gate.

| Security Domain | Verification Method |
|---|---|
| **Facility clearance (FCL)** | DCSA National Industrial Security System (NISS) |
| **NIST 800-171 compliance** | Self-assessment score in DoD SPRS (scored out of 110; negative scores possible) |
| **CMMC certification** | CMMC 2.0 Level 1 (self-assessment) or Level 2 (C3PAO third-party assessment) |
| **FedRAMP authorization** | FedRAMP Marketplace for cloud service offerings |
| **Personnel clearances** | Verify through the firm's FSO; confirm clearance levels match requirement |

For CMMC Level 2, third-party assessment costs for small firms typically range from $30,000 to $100,000+ depending on scope. Evaluators should verify not just certification status, but the scope of the certification boundary and any open Plan of Action and Milestones (POA&M) items.

### Financial Capacity

| Factor | What It Reveals |
|---|---|
| **Annual revenue vs. contract value** | A firm doing $2M/year may struggle to staff a $6M contract without clear financing |
| **Dun & Bradstreet PAYDEX score** | Payment history and creditworthiness |
| **Line of credit / banking references** | Ability to cover payroll and expenses during government payment cycles |
| **Audited financial statements** | Revenue stability, profit margins, cash reserves |
| **Contract concentration** | Dependence on one or two contracts creates risk if either ends |

A common benchmark: a single contract should generally not exceed 40–50% of a small firm's total annual revenue without clear evidence of financial capacity to scale.

### Staffing Model

The staffing model reveals whether a firm's capability is real or borrowed.

- **Personnel accountability** — What matters is whether the firm directly manages and retains its key personnel — not the employment classification. A firm that controls assignments, invests in retention, and can guarantee continuity across the performance period is fundamentally more reliable than one assembling a team through ad-hoc staffing arrangements after award
- **Key personnel commitment** — Are the individuals named in the proposal actually on staff, or subject to "letters of intent" that may not hold?
- **Bench strength** — Can the firm backfill positions if key staff depart?
- **Leadership that has done the work** — Firms where the principals have personally delivered on contracts similar to what they are bidding — not just managed business development — tend to make better staffing decisions, identify risks earlier, and understand the operational realities of the contract environment
- **Limitations on subcontracting** — For set-aside contracts, the small business prime must perform at least **50% of the cost of personnel** for services (per 13 CFR 125.6), though work performed by similarly situated subcontractors can count toward this threshold

## Verification Tools and Databases

Evaluators should cross-reference multiple sources rather than relying on any single database.

| Database | Purpose | Access |
|---|---|---|
| **CPARS** | Past performance evaluations (narrative + ratings) | Government personnel only |
| **SAM.gov** | Entity registration, NAICS codes, certifications, exclusions | Public |
| **FPDS** | Actual contract award data — agencies, dollar values, NAICS | Public |
| **FAPIIS** | Terminations, deficiency reports, integrity issues | Via SAM.gov (review required for awards over $600K per FAR 9.104-6) |
| **SPRS** | NIST 800-171 self-assessment scores | Government personnel (DoD) |
| **USAspending.gov** | Aggregate spending data by vendor | Public |
| **SBA DSBS** | Socioeconomic certification verification | Public |

### CPARS Ratings Scale

Past performance evaluations in CPARS use a five-level rating system:

| Rating | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Exceptional** | Performance significantly exceeded requirements with no quality issues |
| **Very Good** | Performance exceeded some requirements with no significant issues |
| **Satisfactory** | Performance met requirements; some minor problems with no impact on overall performance |
| **Marginal** | Performance did not meet some requirements; remediation required |
| **Unsatisfactory** | Performance failed to meet requirements; unacceptable deficiencies |

For small firms, CPARS data may be incomplete — agencies sometimes delay entering evaluations. Supplement CPARS with direct reference checks and FPDS award verification.

## Evaluating for Prime vs. Subcontractor Roles

The evaluation depth changes depending on the role.

| Factor | Prime Evaluation | Subcontractor Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| **Responsibility determination** | Required (FAR 9.104-1) | Not formally required — prime bears this obligation |
| **Financial capacity** | Must support entire contract value | Must support its share of the work |
| **Management infrastructure** | PM capability, QA processes, contract administration, accounting system | Technical execution focus |
| **Limitations on subcontracting** | Must comply (set-aside contracts) | Not directly applicable |
| **Security clearances** | Facility clearance and personnel clearances required | Personnel clearances for assigned work |
| **Past performance scrutiny** | Full evaluation against all criteria | Assessed as part of the prime's proposal strength |

Under **FAR 44.2**, consent to subcontract may be required for cost-reimbursement, time-and-materials, and labor-hour contracts — giving the CO direct review authority over subcontractor selection.

## The Role of Oral Presentations

**FAR 15.102** authorizes oral presentations as a substitute for, or supplement to, written proposals. For small IT firms, this is a significant equalizer.

Oral presentations allow evaluators to:
- Assess key personnel competence directly, rather than through written resumes
- Distinguish firms with genuine in-house expertise from those relying on proposal consultants
- Probe technical understanding through real-time Q&A
- Reduce the proposal preparation burden that disproportionately affects small firms with limited business development resources

The solicitation must specify format, time limits, topics, and evaluation criteria. Agencies must provide equitable conditions across all offerors.

## Common Evaluation Mistakes

### 1. Overweighting Price

FAR 15.304 permits technical and past performance factors to outweigh price. For complex IT services, they should. The **FY2017 NDAA Section 813** restricts LPTA use for acquisitions where past performance is relevant or requirements are not well-defined — which describes most IT work.

### 2. Ignoring Subcontracting Structure

A small IT prime that subcontracts 80% of the work to a large firm raises **ostensible subcontractor** concerns under 13 CFR 121.103(h)(4). If the subcontractor is performing the primary and vital requirements, the SBA may find affiliation — potentially disqualifying the team on a set-aside.

### 3. Not Verifying Key Personnel Availability

Proposals frequently name individuals who are committed to multiple bids simultaneously. Ask for binding commitment letters and verify availability directly.

### 4. Applying Unstated Evaluation Criteria

Evaluating offerors on factors not disclosed in the solicitation is one of the most common grounds for successful GAO protests. Every evaluation criterion must be stated in the RFP.

### 5. Treating Limited Past Performance as a Negative

FAR 15.305(a)(2)(iv) is clear: no relevant past performance must be rated neutral, not unfavorable. Evaluators who downgrade small firms for being new are violating this provision and creating protest risk.

### 6. Skipping FAPIIS Review

For awards above $600,000, FAR 9.104-6 requires the CO to review FAPIIS data. This check reveals terminations for default, integrity issues, and administrative actions that may not appear in CPARS.

### 7. Evaluating Certifications in Isolation

A firm that holds both WOSB and SDVOSB certifications provides compounding value — it helps the agency credit toward multiple socioeconomic scorecard goals simultaneously and qualifies for both WOSB and SDVOSB set-aside pools. Evaluators who check only one certification box miss the strategic advantage of dual-certified firms that give agencies more acquisition flexibility per teaming relationship.

## A Practical Evaluation Framework

For federal IT source selections involving small businesses, a commonly used weighting structure:

| Factor | Suggested Weight | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical approach / capability** | 30–40% | Determines whether the firm can do the work |
| **Past performance** | 20–30% | Best predictor of future delivery quality |
| **Management approach / staffing** | 15–25% | Assesses organizational capacity and key personnel |
| **Price / cost** | 15–25% | Evaluated for reasonableness and realism, not just lowest number |

Under best-value tradeoff, the technical and past performance factors combined should generally be **significantly more important** than price for complex IT services. This must be stated in the solicitation per FAR 15.304.

## Key Takeaways

- The FAR 9.104-1 responsibility standards are the baseline — every prospective contractor must satisfy all seven
- Past performance neutrality (FAR 15.305(a)(2)(iv)) protects newer small firms from being penalized for limited history
- Cross-reference CPARS, SAM.gov, FPDS, and FAPIIS — no single database gives the full picture
- Security compliance (CMMC, NIST 800-171, FedRAMP) is increasingly a threshold gate, not a scored factor
- Oral presentations under FAR 15.102 level the playing field for small firms with strong technical staff but limited proposal resources
- Firms holding multiple socioeconomic certifications (e.g., both WOSB and SDVOSB) provide compounding scorecard value and access to more set-aside categories per teaming relationship
- Look for firms where leadership has personally delivered on similar contracts — that kind of context cannot be hired after award
- The strongest small IT partners combine verifiable credentials, named personnel, multi-domain delivery capability, and accountable staffing models with financial capacity matched to the contract scope
