Finding a small IT subcontractor is easy. Finding one that can actually deliver on a federal contract — with the right clearances, the right past performance, the right financial capacity, and the right compliance posture — is a different problem entirely.

This guide provides a structured approach for prime contractors who need to identify, vet, and manage small IT subcontractors for federal programs, with particular attention to the regulatory requirements that govern the relationship.

## Why Primes Need Small IT Subcontractors

The requirement is not optional. Under **FAR 19.702**, any contract exceeding **$750,000** ($1.5M for construction) awarded to an other-than-small business must include a subcontracting plan with specific goals for small business participation. But compliance is only one driver — there are four distinct reasons primes engage small IT subcontractors.

### Regulatory Compliance

FAR 52.219-9 requires subcontracting plans with separate percentage goals for **six categories** of small businesses:

| Category | Abbreviation |
|---|---|
| Small Business | SB |
| Small Disadvantaged Business | SDB |
| Women-Owned Small Business | WOSB |
| Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business | SDVOSB |
| Veteran-Owned Small Business | VOSB |
| HUBZone Small Business | HUBZone |

Failure to make a good-faith effort to meet these goals can result in **liquidated damages** under FAR 52.219-16, negative CPARS ratings on the small business subcontracting factor, and reduced competitiveness on future bids.

### Agency Scorecard Pressure

The SBA grades each agency annually on small business goal achievement. Agencies under pressure push that pressure down to their primes. CPARS evaluations now specifically rate contractors on small business subcontracting performance — and those ratings follow you into every future competition.

### Technical Capability

Small IT firms frequently hold niche capabilities in cybersecurity, cloud-native development, AI/ML, data analytics, geospatial platforms, and specialized platform administration that complement a prime's broader program management infrastructure. Firms that span multiple domains — cloud, cybersecurity, analytics, and program support under one team — are particularly valuable because they reduce the integration risk and vendor management overhead of engaging separate single-capability subcontractors. Subcontracting provides access to this expertise without maintaining bench staff year-round.

### Cleared Personnel Access

The cleared IT workforce is limited. Small firms with established facility clearances and cleared personnel offer primes a path to staffing classified work without the timeline risk of sponsoring new clearances.

## Where to Search

### Federal Databases

| Source | Best For | How to Use It |
|---|---|---|
| **SAM.gov** | Verifying active registration, NAICS codes, certifications, exclusions | Search by UEI, CAGE code, NAICS, socioeconomic status, or keywords |
| **SBA Small Business Search** | Filtering by certification type (8(a), WOSB, SDVOSB, HUBZone), geography, and capabilities | Formerly DSBS; now integrated into SAM.gov |
| **SBA SubNet** | Posting subcontracting opportunities for small businesses to find | Demonstrates good faith effort under FAR 19.705-7 |
| **FPDS / USAspending.gov** | Verifying actual contract award history — agencies, dollars, NAICS codes | Confirm a firm's claimed past performance matches real award data |

### Outreach Channels

| Channel | What It Provides |
|---|---|
| **Agency OSDBU offices** | Matchmaking events, referrals, and access to agency-specific small business databases |
| **APEX Accelerators** | Nationwide network of 90+ centers (formerly PTACs) that counsel small businesses on federal contracting; can connect primes with qualified local firms |
| **Industry days** | Pre-solicitation events where primes can identify small businesses already pursuing relevant work |
| **Vendor Outreach Matchmaking Events (VOMEs)** | Structured 15-minute B2B meetings matched by NAICS code and capability area; DHS, GSA, and other agencies host these regularly |
| **Industry associations** | NDIA, AFCEA, SAME, and similar organizations maintain small business directories and host teaming events |

### Demonstrating Good Faith

Participation in these channels is not just good practice — it is evidence. Under the 2021 final rule on good faith compliance (FAR 19.705-7), indicators of good faith effort include conducting market research through all reasonable means, posting on SubNet, attending matchmaking events, and soliciting small businesses early in the acquisition process.

## How to Vet a Potential Subcontractor

### The Verification Checklist

| Check | Source | What You Are Looking For |
|---|---|---|
| **SAM.gov registration** | SAM.gov | Active registration (renewed annually); correct NAICS codes; valid UEI and CAGE code; no exclusions |
| **Size and certification status** | SAM.gov / SBA | Active socioeconomic certifications verified through SBA, not self-reported |
| **Past performance** | CPARS / FPDS | Ratings on prior prime contracts; verified award history matching claimed experience |
| **Financial stability** | Financial statements / D&B | Revenue history relative to proposed subcontract value; adequate cash reserves for 30–60 day government payment cycles |
| **Security posture** | DCSA / NISS | Active facility clearance (FCL) at the required level; personnel clearances for proposed staff |
| **Current contract load** | Direct inquiry | Whether the firm is overcommitted across multiple active contracts |
| **Insurance coverage** | Certificates of insurance | Professional liability, cyber liability, workers' compensation, errors & omissions |
| **Accounting system** | DCAA audit status | Adequate accounting system for cost-type work (DFARS 252.242-7006); may not apply to all IT subcontracts |

### Capability Statement Red Flags

The capability statement is the standard document small businesses use to introduce themselves. Evaluate it critically:

- **No named clients** — vague references to "federal agencies" without specifics indicate limited real experience
- **Capabilities exceed history** — a firm claiming 12 technical domains with 3 years of history cannot deliver all of them at scale
- **No prime contract experience** — while subcontracting experience is valid, a firm with no prime delivery history may lack contract administration maturity
- **Expired certifications or lapsed SAM registration** — indicates the firm is not actively managing its federal compliance posture
- **Generic language** — capability statements that could describe any IT company signal that the firm has not defined its differentiation
- **No evidence of leadership delivery experience** — the strongest small IT firms are led by principals who have personally executed on contracts similar to the ones they bid, not just managed business development. A capability statement that names only corporate credentials without connecting them to the people who delivered the work is less credible

### Reference Checks

Contact prior prime contractors and government CORs who have worked with the firm. Ask specifically about:

- Quality and timeliness of deliverables
- Key personnel stability during the engagement
- Responsiveness to scope changes and surge requirements
- Whether they would work with the firm again — and why

## Structuring the Subcontract

### Flow-Down Clauses

Prime contractors must flow down certain FAR clauses to subcontractors. The two most relevant for small business subcontracting:

- **FAR 52.219-8** (Utilization of Small Business Concerns) — must be included in all subcontracts that offer further subcontracting opportunities
- **FAR 52.219-9** (Small Business Subcontracting Plan) — must be flowed down to other-than-small subcontractors receiving subcontracts exceeding $750,000 with further subcontracting possibilities

Additional mandatory flow-downs depend on the contract type but commonly include cybersecurity requirements (DFARS 252.204-7012 for CUI handling), equal opportunity, anti-kickback provisions, and CAS applicability.

### Payment Terms

The **Prompt Payment Act** establishes clear requirements:

- Once the prime receives payment from the government, it has **7 calendar days** to pay subcontractors for satisfactory performance
- If payment is withheld, the prime must provide **written notice within 7 days** of invoice receipt, specifying the amount withheld, the reason, and corrective actions needed
- Interest penalties accrue automatically for late payments
- FAR 19.704(a)(15) requires the prime to assure that it will pay small business subcontractors on time and notify the CO of any reduced or untimely payments

### Performance Monitoring

Define clear deliverables, milestones, and acceptance criteria in the subcontract. Establish quality assurance procedures that mirror the prime contract's QASP. Include provisions for:

- Regular performance reviews and progress reporting
- Key personnel requirements with approval and replacement clauses
- Remedies for non-performance — cure notices, stop-work authority, and termination provisions
- Earned value metrics for larger subcontracts

## The Similarly Situated Entity Rule

Under **13 CFR 125.6**, work performed by a **similarly situated entity** counts toward the small business prime's self-performance requirement on set-aside contracts. This matters because your small business subcontractors may win their own set-aside primes and need to find their own qualified subs.

A similarly situated entity is a first-tier subcontractor that:
1. Has the **same small business program status** as the prime (e.g., SDVOSB sub under an SDVOSB prime)
2. Is **small under the NAICS size standard** assigned to the subcontract

For a general small business set-aside, any small business qualifies. For an SDVOSB set-aside, the similarly situated sub must also be SDVOSB. Work that a similarly situated entity further subcontracts to a non-similarly-situated firm counts **against** the prime's self-performance threshold.

## Reporting Requirements

### The Two Reports

| Report | Frequency | Scope | System |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Individual Subcontracting Report (ISR)** | Semi-annually (periods ending March 31 and September 30) + within 30 days of contract completion | One ISR per contract | SAM.gov (formerly eSRS) |
| **Summary Subcontracting Report (SSR)** | Annually, within 30 days after the end of the government fiscal year (by October 30) | One SSR covers the contractor's entire subcontracting activity across all contracts | SAM.gov (formerly eSRS) |

### The eSRS Migration

As of **February 2026**, the Electronic Subcontracting Reporting System (eSRS.gov) has been retired. All subcontracting reporting now occurs through **SAM.gov**. Key changes:

- Only one report permitted per Procurement Instrument Identification (PIID)
- AI-based review and anomaly reports replace the prior CO acknowledgement workflow
- NAICS code reporting is no longer required in the new system
- FPDS data also migrated to SAM.gov as of February 2026

### Enforcement Reality

A GAO report (GAO-24-106225) found that for 14 of 26 contracts reviewed, contracting officers did not ensure required subcontracting reports were submitted. Most agencies do not track agency-level data on whether contractors are meeting their small business goals. SBA conducted only 6 compliance reviews of subcontracting plans in each of FY2021 and FY2022.

This enforcement gap does not mean compliance is optional — it means that primes who do comply build credibility that their competitors may lack.

## The Good Faith Effort Standard

Failing to meet subcontracting goals does **not**, by itself, constitute a failure to make good faith effort. Under **FAR 19.705-7**, the CO evaluates the **totality of the contractor's actions** — and the standard is whether the contractor made a willful or intentional failure to perform in accordance with the plan.

### What Demonstrates Good Faith

| Indicator | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Breaking out work into economically feasible units for small businesses | Shows active effort to create subcontracting opportunities |
| Conducting market research through SAM.gov, SubNet, and DSBS | Demonstrates use of all available tools to find qualified firms |
| Attending matchmaking events and industry days | Shows proactive engagement beyond database searches |
| Soliciting small businesses early in the acquisition process | Not waiting until the last minute to find subs |
| Providing adequate information about requirements to interested firms | Giving small businesses enough detail to bid competitively |
| Participating in the Mentor-Protégé program | Strongest indicator of long-term commitment |
| Exceeding goals in one period despite shortfalls in another | Shows sustained effort even if results vary |

### What Demonstrates Non-Compliance

| Indicator | Risk |
|---|---|
| No market research attempts to identify small businesses | Strongest indicator of failure |
| No designated plan administrator | Suggests the plan exists on paper only |
| Late or missing subcontracting reports | Administrative non-compliance |
| Failure to pay small business subs per contract terms | Direct harm to small businesses; CO notification required |
| Failure to maintain compliance records | Cannot demonstrate good faith if challenged |
| Falsifying records of subcontract awards | Fraud — potential debarment |

### Enforcement Consequences

If the CO determines the contractor failed to make good faith effort:

1. The contractor receives written notice specifying the material breach
2. The contractor has **15 working days** to respond and demonstrate good faith efforts
3. If the CO finds failure after reviewing all data, the CO issues a final decision requiring **liquidated damages** — calculated as the actual dollar shortfall from each subcontracting goal
4. The contractor may also receive an adverse CPARS rating on the subcontracting factor
5. The contractor can appeal under the Disputes clause

## Common Problems and How to Avoid Them

### Key Personnel Turnover

Small IT firms have less bench depth. Cleared IT professionals are in high demand, and small firms frequently lose staff to larger competitors. **Mitigation**: Include key personnel clauses with replacement approval rights, and evaluate the firm's retention track record during vetting.

### Overcommitment

Small firms may bid aggressively across multiple contracts, committing the same personnel to overlapping engagements. **Mitigation**: Ask for the firm's current contract load during vetting. Verify that proposed personnel are not already committed to other active contracts.

### Inability to Scale

Small IT firms may lack the recruiting infrastructure or financial reserves to rapidly scale staffing for surge requirements. **Mitigation**: Assess ramp-up capability during vetting. Understand the firm's recruiting pipeline and whether it can carry unfunded personnel during transition periods.

### Clearance Gaps

New clearance processing takes 6–18 months. A firm may hold an FCL but lack sufficient cleared personnel for the specific work. **Mitigation**: Verify both facility clearance status and individual clearance levels for proposed personnel before finalizing the teaming arrangement.

### Compliance Gaps

Small firms may lack mature accounting systems (DCAA-compliant), property management systems, or cybersecurity frameworks (CMMC). **Mitigation**: For cost-type subcontracts, confirm accounting system adequacy before award. For CUI-handling work, verify NIST 800-171 compliance posture.

## Building a Long-Term Subcontracting Pipeline

The most effective primes treat small business subcontracting as an ongoing capability development program, not a per-contract compliance exercise.

- **Maintain a vendor reference file** organized by NAICS code, certification type, clearance level, and capability area
- **Provide formal performance feedback** — under 13 CFR 125.11, primes are required to provide past performance ratings to first-tier small business subs within **15 days** of request, using the standard five-tier CPARS scale
- **Help subs build past performance** by assigning meaningful, independently identifiable scope rather than generic staff augmentation roles
- **Consider the Mentor-Protégé program** for high-potential small business partners — the developmental relationship builds a more capable, more loyal teaming partner
- **Prioritize dual-certified subcontractors** — a subcontractor holding both WOSB and SDVOSB certifications allows the prime to count the same subcontract dollars toward two separate subcontracting plan categories, which is a significant compliance efficiency when managing multiple socioeconomic targets
- **Share contract intelligence** — when you see upcoming requirements that fit a sub's capabilities, alert them early so they can prepare

## Key Takeaways

- Subcontracting plans are mandatory for contracts over $750,000 and must include goals for six small business categories
- Use SAM.gov, SBA Small Business Search, SubNet, APEX Accelerators, and agency OSDBU events to find qualified firms — and document your search efforts as evidence of good faith
- Vet subcontractors rigorously: verify SAM registration, check CPARS and FPDS, confirm clearances, assess financial capacity, and call references
- Flow down FAR 52.219-8 and FAR 52.219-9 as required; pay within 7 days of receiving government payment
- Subcontracting reporting has moved from eSRS to SAM.gov as of February 2026
- The good faith standard evaluates the totality of your actions — missing a goal is not a violation, but failing to try is
- Dual-certified subcontractors (e.g., both WOSB and SDVOSB) let the prime count one relationship toward multiple subcontracting plan goals — a significant efficiency for firms managing several socioeconomic targets
- The strongest subcontracting programs build long-term pipelines of qualified small IT firms with multi-domain capability and accountable staffing — not one-off compliance arrangements with single-niche vendors
