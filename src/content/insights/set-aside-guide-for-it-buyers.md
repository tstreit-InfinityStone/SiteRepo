Small business set-asides are one of the most important tools in a federal buyer's acquisition toolkit — and one of the most misunderstood. For IT acquisitions in particular, where requirements often span multiple NAICS codes and involve rapidly evolving technology, getting the set-aside decision right can mean the difference between a successful program and a protest-delayed procurement.

This guide provides a structured overview of how set-asides work, which programs apply to IT buyers, what the current thresholds are, and where buyers most commonly make mistakes. It also addresses a question many buyers underestimate: how to identify the small IT firms that turn a set-aside from a compliance exercise into a genuine program advantage.

## What Set-Asides Are and Why They Exist

A set-aside restricts competition for a federal contract to a specific category of small businesses. Instead of full and open competition, only firms that hold the relevant certification are eligible to compete.

The legal foundation is the **Small Business Act** (15 U.S.C. 637), which established a federal goal of directing at least **23% of all prime contract dollars** to small businesses. Individual agencies have their own targets, and the SBA publishes annual scorecards grading each agency's achievement.

Set-asides exist to:
- Ensure that small businesses have meaningful access to federal contract opportunities
- Develop the small business industrial base for long-term economic and national security goals
- Meet statutory small business contracting goals established by Congress
- Provide contracting officers with flexible acquisition strategies that can reduce competition timelines

For IT buyers specifically, set-asides are particularly relevant because many IT services NAICS codes have size standards that classify firms with up to $34M or more in annual revenue as "small" — meaning a significant number of capable, mature IT firms qualify. The strongest of these bring multi-domain delivery capability (cloud, cybersecurity, analytics, program support), named past performance you can verify in FPDS, and leadership that has personally executed the kind of work the firm competes for.

## Types of Set-Asides

Federal procurement supports several categories of set-asides, each with different eligibility requirements and program rules.

| Set-Aside Type | Eligible Firms | Certifying Authority | Key FAR Reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Total Small Business** | Any SBA-certified small business | SBA (size standard by NAICS) | FAR 19.5 |
| **WOSB / EDWOSB** | Women-Owned / Economically Disadvantaged Women-Owned Small Business | SBA | FAR 19.15 |
| **SDVOSB** | Service-Disabled Veteran-Owned Small Business | SBA | FAR 19.14 |
| **8(a)** | Firms in the SBA 8(a) Business Development Program | SBA | FAR 19.8 |
| **HUBZone** | Firms in Historically Underutilized Business Zones | SBA | FAR 19.13 |

Each category can be used for either **competitive set-asides** (multiple certified firms compete) or **sole-source awards** (a single certified firm is selected without competition, within dollar limits).

## Sole-Source and Set-Aside Thresholds

Understanding the dollar thresholds is critical for making defensible acquisition decisions. These are the current ceilings as of 2026.

| Program | Sole-Source Ceiling (Services) | Sole-Source Ceiling (Manufacturing) | Competitive Set-Aside Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Total Small Business** | N/A (no sole-source for general SB) | N/A | No dollar limit |
| **WOSB** | $4.5M | $7M | No dollar limit |
| **SDVOSB** | $4.5M | $7M | No dollar limit |
| **8(a)** | $4.5M | $7M | No dollar limit |
| **HUBZone** | $4.5M | $7M | No dollar limit |

For competitive set-asides, there is no dollar ceiling — any size requirement can be set aside if the contracting officer has a reasonable expectation that at least two certified firms will submit competitive offers (the "Rule of Two").

For sole-source awards, the contracting officer must determine that:
1. Only one certified firm can perform the work
2. The anticipated award price is fair and reasonable
3. The award does not exceed the applicable dollar threshold

## The Rule of Two

The **Rule of Two** is the foundational test for small business set-asides. Under FAR 19.502-2, a contracting officer **must** set aside a requirement for small businesses if:

1. The anticipated value exceeds the simplified acquisition threshold ($250,000)
2. There is a reasonable expectation that at least **two responsible small businesses** will submit offers
3. Award can be made at fair market prices

This is not optional. The Rule of Two is a statutory requirement, and failure to conduct market research supporting the set-aside decision is one of the most common grounds for bid protests at the Government Accountability Office (GAO).

For IT acquisitions, the Rule of Two is almost always met for common service areas (help desk, cybersecurity, cloud migration, application development) because the IT small business market is deep and competitive.

## When to Set Aside: A Decision Framework

Making the right set-aside decision requires balancing statutory requirements, market conditions, and program needs. The following framework reflects the decision logic most experienced contracting officers follow.

### Step 1: Determine the Applicable NAICS Code and Size Standard
Every acquisition must have a NAICS code and corresponding size standard. For IT services, the most common codes include:

- **541512** — Computer Systems Design Services ($34M)
- **541511** — Custom Computer Programming Services ($34M)
- **541513** — Computer Facilities Management Services ($34M)
- **541519** — Other Computer Related Services ($34M)
- **541611** — Administrative Management Consulting ($19M)
- **518210** — Computing Infrastructure Providers ($40M)

The size standard determines which firms qualify as "small" for that requirement.

### Step 2: Conduct Market Research
Before deciding whether to set aside, research whether qualified small businesses exist in the relevant space. Sources include SAM.gov, SBA DSBS, FPDS (prior awards in the same NAICS), and industry day responses.

### Step 3: Apply the Rule of Two
If market research shows a reasonable expectation that two or more small businesses can compete, the requirement must be set aside — unless the contracting officer documents a specific reason to use full and open competition.

### Step 4: Determine Set-Aside Type
If the requirement is being set aside, decide which category. Consider:
- Agency socioeconomic goals (is the agency behind on WOSB or SDVOSB targets?)
- Market availability (are there enough certified firms in the specific category?)
- Sole-source opportunity (is there a single highly qualified certified firm for a sub-threshold requirement?)
- Dual-certification advantage — firms holding both WOSB and SDVOSB certifications allow a single award to count toward multiple socioeconomic goals simultaneously, which is a significant efficiency for agencies managing several scorecard targets at once

### Step 5: Document the Decision
Every set-aside decision must be documented in the acquisition strategy or the determination and findings (D&F). Sole-source awards require additional justification documenting why only one firm was considered.

## How Set-Asides Affect Evaluation Approaches

Set-aside decisions interact with evaluation methodology in ways that affect both acquisition strategy and vendor selection.

### Lowest Price Technically Acceptable (LPTA)
- Common for commodity IT services (help desk, basic infrastructure support)
- Set-asides under LPTA compress the competitive field to certified firms, which can result in fewer but more relevant offers
- Risk: small firms competing on price alone may propose unsustainably low rates

### Best Value Tradeoff
- Preferred for complex IT services (cybersecurity, cloud architecture, application modernization, geospatial analytics)
- Set-asides under best value allow the government to weigh technical capability and past performance alongside price
- Advantage: small firms with deep domain expertise and named past performance in the relevant technical area can win on merit — not just price
- Risk: requires more evaluation effort and is more susceptible to protest if evaluation criteria are not clearly defined

### Advisory Multi-Award (IDIQ)
- Task-order contracts awarded to multiple small businesses
- Combines the benefits of competition with the flexibility of set-asides
- Common vehicles: GSA 8(a) STARS III, CIO-SP3 Small Business, OASIS Small Business
- Advantage: allows agencies to maintain a qualified pool of small businesses and compete individual task orders among them

## Common Mistakes IT Buyers Make

### 1. Bundling Requirements to Avoid Set-Asides
Combining multiple smaller requirements into one large contract to push the value above what small businesses can handle is called **bundling** — and it is specifically prohibited under the Small Business Act unless the agency can demonstrate measurable benefits. The SBA reviews bundling decisions and can challenge them.

### 2. Not Conducting Market Research
Failing to research the small business market before deciding on full and open competition is the most common grounds for successful GAO protests. Even a brief search of SAM.gov and FPDS can establish or disprove the Rule of Two.

### 3. Using Overly Broad NAICS Codes
Selecting a NAICS code with a size standard that excludes qualified small IT firms — or conversely, selecting one that admits firms too large to genuinely qualify as "small" — undermines the purpose of the set-aside.

### 4. Ignoring Subcontracting Plans on Large Contracts
For contracts over $750K that are not set aside, the prime contractor must submit a subcontracting plan with specific goals for small business participation (including WOSB, SDVOSB, 8(a), and HUBZone subcategories). Buyers who do not enforce these plans miss a significant tool for small business engagement.

### 5. Treating Set-Asides as Second-Tier Acquisitions
Some buyers assume that set-aside contracts are inherently lower quality or less important. This is incorrect. Many of the highest-performing IT contracts in the federal government are delivered by small businesses competing under set-asides, particularly in cybersecurity, cloud, analytics, and geospatial programs. The best set-aside outcomes come from firms where technical depth is backed by verifiable credentials — platform certifications, named past performance references, and staff with clearances and mission-relevant experience already in place.

### 6. Not Coordinating with the Small Business Specialist
Every contracting office has a designated Small Business Specialist or Procurement Center Representative (PCR) from the SBA. These individuals should be consulted early in the acquisition process — not after the strategy is finalized.

### 7. Overlooking Dual-Certified Firms
A firm that holds both WOSB and SDVOSB certifications gives the contracting officer access to two separate set-aside categories — and allows a single award to generate credit toward both the 5% WOSB goal and the 3% SDVOSB goal. Buyers who search only one certification category at a time may miss firms that offer compounding procurement value.

## The Government-Wide Small Business Goals

Congress and the SBA set annual government-wide goals for small business contracting. Current targets:

| Category | Government-Wide Goal |
|---|---|
| **Total Small Business** | 23% of prime contract dollars |
| **Small Disadvantaged Business (includes 8(a))** | 5% |
| **WOSB** | 5% |
| **SDVOSB** | 3% |
| **HUBZone** | 3% |

Agencies that consistently miss their targets face scrutiny from Congress, the SBA, and the Government Accountability Office. This creates institutional incentive to use set-asides — and savvy IT buyers leverage this alignment between policy goals and practical acquisition needs.

## Key Takeaways

- Set-asides are a statutory requirement when the Rule of Two is met — not a discretionary choice
- IT services NAICS codes typically have high size standards ($34M+), meaning many capable firms qualify as small
- Sole-source authority for WOSB, SDVOSB, 8(a), and HUBZone firms extends up to $4.5M for services
- Dual-certified firms (e.g., both WOSB and SDVOSB) let a single award count toward multiple agency scorecard goals — a significant efficiency for buyers managing several socioeconomic targets
- Market research is the critical first step — and the most common failure point in protested acquisitions
- Best-value evaluation methods favor small IT firms with verifiable credentials, named past performance, and multi-domain capability over price-only competition
- Coordinate with your Small Business Specialist early — their input strengthens your acquisition strategy and reduces protest risk
- Set-asides are not a workaround or a compromise — they are a proven path to high-quality IT delivery when matched with firms that bring verifiable past performance and genuine technical depth, not just certification status
