If you are a small IT firm pursuing federal contracts, the contracting officer is the person who decides whether you get the work. Understanding how COs evaluate vendors — what builds confidence, what triggers hesitation, and what gets a proposal set aside without a second look — is the difference between winning and wondering why you lost.

This guide walks through the CO's evaluation process from the inside: the regulatory framework, the databases they check, the factors they weigh, and the mistakes that cause small firms to be passed over.

## The Responsibility Determination: The First Gate

Before any contract can be awarded, the CO must make an affirmative determination of responsibility under **FAR 9.104-1**. This is a pass/fail gate — not a scored evaluation, but a binary decision that the prospective contractor meets minimum standards.

### The Seven General Standards

| Standard | What the CO Assesses |
|---|---|
| **Adequate financial resources** | Can the firm fund operations through the contract period, or demonstrate access to financing? |
| **Delivery/performance schedule** | Can the firm meet timelines given its existing commitments? |
| **Satisfactory performance record** | Does CPARS, FAPIIS, or other data show acceptable prior performance? |
| **Integrity and business ethics** | Any debarments, suspensions, tax delinquencies, or legal proceedings? |
| **Organization and experience** | Does the firm have the management structure, accounting systems, and operational controls needed? |
| **Technical equipment and facilities** | Does the firm have — or can it obtain — the infrastructure to perform? |
| **Otherwise qualified and eligible** | Is the firm legally eligible under all applicable statutes and regulations? |

The phrase **"or the ability to obtain them"** appears repeatedly in these standards. This is critical for small businesses — a firm does not need to possess every resource at proposal time. It must demonstrate the credible ability to acquire them before performance begins.

If a CO finds a small business non-responsible, the matter must be referred to the **SBA for a Certificate of Competency** under FAR 19.602. The SBA can override the CO's determination and compel the award. This is a significant protection that many small firms do not know exists.

## Where COs Look: The Databases

COs do not evaluate vendors in a vacuum. They cross-reference multiple federal systems before making award decisions.

### SAM.gov

Every CO checks SAM.gov first. An expired or incomplete registration is an immediate disqualifier — no award can be made to an unregistered entity (FAR 4.1102).

What COs verify:
- **Active registration status** — must be current, renewed annually
- **NAICS codes** — do the firm's listed codes match the solicitation?
- **Representations and certifications** — small business size status, socioeconomic designations (8(a), WOSB, SDVOSB, HUBZone)
- **Exclusions** — any debarments, suspensions, or proposed debarments are an absolute bar to award
- **UEI (Unique Entity Identifier)** — must match across all proposal documents

### CPARS (Contractor Performance Assessment Reporting System)

CPARS is the government-wide system where COs and CORs enter performance evaluations. Per FAR 42.1502, evaluations are required for contracts exceeding the simplified acquisition threshold ($250,000).

COs search by UEI, CAGE code, or company name and review ratings across multiple areas: quality, schedule, cost control, management, and small business subcontracting compliance.

| Rating | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **Exceptional** | Performance significantly exceeded requirements with few minor problems |
| **Very Good** | Performance exceeded some requirements with no significant issues |
| **Satisfactory** | Performance met requirements; minor problems with no overall impact |
| **Marginal** | Performance did not meet some requirements; corrective action needed |
| **Unsatisfactory** | Performance failed to meet requirements; recovery unlikely |

COs read the **narrative comments** as much as the ratings — a "Satisfactory" rating with glowing narrative detail carries more weight than a bare rating. They also look for **trends**: improving performance suggests corrective capacity, while declining performance is a red flag.

For firms with **no past performance record**, FAR 15.305(a)(2)(iv) requires neutral treatment — the absence of history cannot be scored negatively. But in a competitive best-value evaluation, a firm with strong relevant past performance will still score higher.

### FAPIIS (Federal Awardee Performance and Integrity Information System)

For awards above $250,000, FAR 9.104-6 requires the CO to review FAPIIS before making a responsibility determination. FAPIIS reveals what CPARS does not:

- Terminations for default or cause
- Deficiency reports and corrective action requests
- Non-responsibility determinations by other COs
- Criminal, civil, and administrative proceedings disclosed under FAR 52.209-7
- Tax delinquencies

A FAPIIS entry does not automatically disqualify a firm, but the CO must consider the information and document how it factored into the decision.

### SPRS (Supplier Performance Risk System)

For DoD contracts involving Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI), COs check the contractor's **NIST 800-171 self-assessment score** in SPRS. The maximum score is 110. Scores can go negative (down to -203) if many controls are not implemented.

**A current SPRS score must be posted before contract award** — no score means no award for DoD contracts requiring DFARS 252.204-7019/7020 compliance.

## How Technical Evaluation Works

The evaluation method determines how much your technical capability matters relative to price.

### LPTA (Lowest Price Technically Acceptable)

Under **FAR 15.101-2**, proposals are evaluated against stated technical requirements on a pass/fail basis. Among all "acceptable" proposals, the lowest price wins.

- Technical excellence above the threshold provides **no advantage**
- A single "unacceptable" technical element typically renders the entire proposal unacceptable
- Section 813 of the FY2017 NDAA restricts LPTA use for DoD IT acquisitions where quality differentiation matters

### Best Value Tradeoff

Under **FAR 15.101-1**, the source selection authority can accept a higher-priced proposal if technical superiority justifies the premium. This is where small IT firms with deep domain expertise have their strongest advantage.

Common evaluation factors for IT contracts:

| Factor | Typical Weight | What COs Evaluate |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical approach** | 30–40% | Understanding of the problem, proposed methodology, innovation, feasibility |
| **Management / staffing** | 15–25% | Key personnel qualifications, organizational structure, transition plan, surge capacity |
| **Past performance** | 20–30% | Relevancy, recency, quality of prior work on similar contracts |
| **Price / cost** | 15–25% | Reasonableness, realism, completeness — not just lowest number |

FAR 15.304(e) requires the solicitation to disclose the relative importance. For complex IT services, technical and past performance combined are typically **significantly more important** than price.

## Security and Compliance: The Threshold Gates

For IT contracts, security compliance is increasingly a pass/fail prerequisite rather than a scored evaluation factor.

### What COs Check

| Requirement | Verification Method | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Facility clearance (FCL)** | DCSA National Industrial Security System (NISS) | Required for classified work — no clearance means no eligibility |
| **NIST 800-171 compliance** | SPRS self-assessment score | Score must be posted before DoD award |
| **CMMC certification** | CMMC 2.0 — Level 1 (self-assessment) or Level 2 (C3PAO assessment) | Being phased into solicitations starting 2025 |
| **FedRAMP authorization** | FedRAMP Marketplace | Required for cloud service offerings used by federal agencies |
| **Personnel clearances** | Verified through FSO | Must match clearance requirements in the DD-254 |

**CMMC Level 2** third-party assessment costs for small firms typically range from $30,000 to $100,000+. COs cannot waive this requirement once it appears in the solicitation, but they recognize the burden it places on small businesses.

## The Small Business Specialist and PCR

Every contracting activity has a **Small Business Specialist** (FAR 19.201), and the SBA assigns **Procurement Center Representatives (PCRs)** to major federal buying offices (FAR 19.402).

These individuals review proposed acquisitions and recommend whether to set aside for small businesses. If a PCR believes a requirement should be set aside and the CO disagrees, the PCR can **formally appeal** under FAR 19.505 — triggering a review by the SBA Administrator and the agency head.

For small IT vendors, this means someone inside the acquisition process is specifically looking for opportunities to direct work to qualified small businesses. Engaging with your agency's **OSDBU (Office of Small and Disadvantaged Business Utilization)** can connect you with these advocates.

## What Makes a CO Confident

COs evaluate hundreds of proposals. The firms that stand out share common characteristics:

- **Directly relevant past performance** on contracts of similar size, scope, and technology — with named agencies and verifiable contract numbers
- **Named key personnel** with specific, relevant experience — not generic resumes with "TBD" assignments
- **A detailed, realistic technical approach** that demonstrates understanding of the actual problem, not a restated PWS
- **A clear staffing and management plan** showing ramp-up strategy, turnover mitigation, and surge capacity
- **Active security clearances** already in place — facility and personnel
- **Clean CPARS history** with Satisfactory or better ratings
- **SPRS score at or near 110** with a credible POA&M for any gaps
- **Responsive, professional engagement** during the pre-award process — attending industry days, asking informed questions, submitting complete and on-time responses

## What Makes a CO Hesitant

These are the patterns that cause COs to lose confidence in a small IT firm:

- **Key personnel listed as "available upon award"** — signals the firm does not actually have the staff
- **Generic proposals** that restate the requirements rather than explaining how the firm will meet them
- **No prime contract experience** — only subcontractor history, with no evidence of ability to manage the full contract administration burden
- **Contract value far exceeds annual revenue** — a $1M firm bidding on a $8M contract without clear financing raises cash flow concerns
- **Over-reliance on a single subcontractor** — raises pass-through and ostensible subcontractor affiliation concerns under 13 CFR 121.103(h)(4)
- **FAPIIS entries** without evidence of corrective action
- **Low or missing SPRS score** with an unrealistic POA&M
- **Copy-paste proposals** that reference the wrong agency or contract — this happens more often than anyone admits

## Common Reasons Small IT Firms Get Passed Over

### 1. Poor Proposal Quality
Failure to address all evaluation factors, non-compliance with Section L formatting instructions, mathematical errors in pricing, or proposals that read like marketing brochures instead of technical responses. The proposal is your primary evaluation artifact — treat it accordingly.

### 2. No Relevant Past Performance
Performing IT support for a private company is not equivalent to delivering IT services under federal contract requirements. Similarly, past performance on a $500K contract may not demonstrate capability for a $10M engagement. COs evaluate relevancy based on scope, magnitude, complexity, and domain.

### 3. Unclear Staffing Plans
Staffing plans that assume 100% utilization, zero turnover, or rely entirely on post-award recruiting. COs view unrealistic staffing assumptions as evidence that the firm does not understand the operational demands of the contract.

### 4. Limitations on Subcontracting Concerns
Under **FAR 52.219-14**, small business primes must perform at least **50% of the cost of personnel** for service contracts. COs evaluate whether the firm is genuinely performing the work or acting as a pass-through. A proposal where the subcontractor performs the primary and vital requirements will raise red flags.

### 5. Inadequate Accounting Systems
For cost-reimbursement contracts, DCAA audit of the accounting system is required (DFARS 252.242-7006). Many small firms fail this requirement. If your firm cannot pass an accounting system adequacy review, cost-type contracts are effectively off limits.

### 6. Pricing Problems
Pricing that is unrealistically low suggests misunderstanding of scope or a buy-in strategy (FAR 15.404-1(d) requires cost realism analysis for cost-type contracts). Pricing that is unreasonably high relative to the IGCE raises different concerns. Unbalanced pricing across CLINs creates risk for both parties.

## How to Position Your Firm

Understanding the CO's evaluation lens allows small IT firms to position themselves more effectively:

- **Build prime contract experience** — even on smaller contracts — to demonstrate full lifecycle delivery capability
- **Invest in CPARS-worthy performance** on every engagement; your next evaluator will read the narrative
- **Keep SAM.gov registration current** and accurate — an expired registration at proposal time is an unforced error
- **Post your SPRS score** before pursuing DoD work; begin CMMC preparation early
- **Name your people** — proposals with specific, committed key personnel score higher than those with placeholders
- **Write to the evaluation criteria** — every sentence in your proposal should map to a factor in Section M
- **Engage early** — attend industry days, respond to RFIs, meet with the OSDBU, and ask informed questions that demonstrate domain understanding

## Key Takeaways

- The responsibility determination (FAR 9.104-1) is the first gate — COs must verify all seven general standards before award
- CPARS, SAM.gov, FAPIIS, and SPRS are mandatory checkpoints — ensure your data in each system is current and accurate
- Best-value tradeoff evaluations favor small IT firms with deep technical expertise and relevant past performance over lowest-price competitors
- Security compliance (NIST 800-171, CMMC, facility clearances) is increasingly a threshold gate, not a scored differentiator
- The SBA's Certificate of Competency process protects small businesses from non-responsibility determinations — know your rights
- COs gain confidence from specificity: named personnel, named clients, named platforms, and realistic plans backed by verifiable evidence
